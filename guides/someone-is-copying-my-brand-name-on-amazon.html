You search your own brand on Amazon and find another seller using the same name, or one that's a letter off, on products like yours. It feels like theft, and sometimes it is. What you can actually do about it depends mostly on one question: what trademark rights can you prove? This guide walks through that question first, then Amazon's tools, then what happens outside Amazon.

Step 1: Write down what you're seeing

Before you report anything, capture the evidence. Save the listing URLs and ASINs, take dated screenshots of the listing title, brand field, images and seller name, and note whether they're selling on your own listing or on a separate one. Also note how close the name is and how close the products are. The USPTO says the key factors in most infringement cases are how similar the marks are and whether the goods are related enough that shoppers would assume they come from the same source. Courts also look at things like where the products are sold, who buys them, any actual confusion and the other party's intent (USPTO, About trademark infringement).

That means "they use the same word" isn't the whole test. A similar name on unrelated products may not be a problem. A near-identical name on the same kind of product in the same store is much closer to the line. Customer messages or reviews that mix the two brands up are worth saving, since actual confusion is one of the factors.

Step 2: Figure out what rights you actually have

Search your own name in the USPTO trademark search. You're in one of three places:

  • You have a federal registration. The USPTO says a registration on the Principal Register gives a legal presumption that the mark is valid, that you own it and that you have the exclusive right to use it nationwide for the goods or services listed (USPTO). This is the strongest position.
  • You have a pending application. That's enough to enroll in Amazon Brand Registry (more below), but it isn't a registration yet.
  • You've never filed. You may still have common law rights from using the name to sell. The USPTO says those rights start when you use the mark with your goods or services, but they only apply to the geographic area where you're providing them. A federal registration is what creates nationwide rights (USPTO, What is a trademark?).

Also search the other seller's brand. If they've filed a US application for your name, you're dealing with a different problem, and the timing matters. See our guide on what to do when someone files a trademark on your shop name, and the one on letters of protest vs. opposition.

Step 3: Use Amazon's tools

Amazon's main brand-protection program is Brand Registry. Amazon lists the requirements to enroll (Amazon Brand Registry, Eligibility):

  • A logo that includes your brand name, permanently affixed to your products or packaging.
  • A pending or registered trademark for your brand name or logo, issued by the government trademark office of a country with an Amazon store. For a US brand, that's the USPTO.
  • You must be the trademark owner, and the trademark must be a word mark or a design mark with words, letters or numbers. The trademark text has to match the brand name on your application.
  • Your trademark registration or application number.

Once enrolled, Amazon says you can use its Report a Violation tool to report suspected IP infringement and other policy problems in one place. Amazon also says it offers a publicly available form for reporting alleged copyright, trademark and patent infringement, which doesn't require Brand Registry (Amazon).

Keep reports accurate. Amazon says accurate submissions help it block violations automatically. A report that claims a trademark you don't own, or covers products that aren't actually confusing, can come back on you. If the other seller is offering your actual product on your listing, that's usually a different issue from trademark copying, and an attorney can tell you which claim fits.

Step 4: If Amazon reporting doesn't fix it

The USPTO won't step in for you. It says it only registers trademarks, and that you, as the owner, are solely responsible for enforcement, including bringing any legal action to stop someone using an infringing mark (USPTO, Trademark process).

The usual next step is a demand letter, often called a cease and desist, sent by you or your attorney. If that fails, a trademark owner can file a lawsuit, usually in federal court. If infringement is proven, the USPTO lists possible remedies including an order to stop using the mark, destruction of infringing goods, the defendant's profits and damages, and in some cases attorneys' fees (USPTO). That's expensive, so it's worth getting an attorney's honest read on your evidence first. If you're on the receiving end of a letter instead, see our cease and desist guide.

If the copied goods are being imported, the USPTO notes that owners of a registered trademark can record it with U.S. Customs and Border Protection through CBP's e-Recordation application (USPTO).

Step 5: Close the gap for next time

If you've never filed, this is often the moment sellers decide to. A pending application unlocks Brand Registry, and a registration gives you the nationwide presumptions above. The USPTO fee schedule lists a $350 base application fee per class for electronic filing, with possible surcharges. Copycats on Amazon sometimes go one step further and file their own trademark on a name like yours, which can let them enroll in Brand Registry. FAQ

Can I report a copycat on Amazon without a trademark?
Amazon has a public form for reporting alleged IP infringement, but Brand Registry and its Report a Violation tool require a pending or registered trademark (Amazon). Without a filing, you're relying on common law rights, which are limited to the area where you sell (USPTO).
What does Amazon Brand Registry require?
Your brand name or logo permanently on your products or packaging, plus a pending or registered trademark that you own, with text matching your brand name (Amazon).
Will the USPTO stop the other seller?
No. The USPTO says owners are solely responsible for enforcing their trademarks (USPTO).
Does the other seller have to use the exact same name?
No. Infringement turns on likelihood of confusion, and the main factors are how similar the marks are and how related the goods are (USPTO).
How much does it cost to file a US trademark?
The USPTO lists a $350 base fee per class for electronic filing, with possible surcharges (USPTO fee schedule).
