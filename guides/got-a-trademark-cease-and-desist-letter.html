What a cease and desist letter means

The USPTO describes a cease and desist letter as correspondence that says or suggests you might be infringing someone else's trademark and asks you to stop using the mark. It also says to treat any such letter seriously (USPTO, I received a letter/email).

Getting one doesn't mean you've been sued. If a federal case is filed, the trademark owner generally has to serve you with a complaint and a summons from the court clerk, usually by handing them to you or to your business's registered agent. That's why you'll usually know when you've actually been sued (USPTO). If papers like that do show up, the clock is short. Under the federal rules a defendant generally has 21 days after being served to answer (FRCP 12). Call an attorney that day.

Check the claim yourself in USPTO records

A lot of letters cite a registration number. Look it up in TSDR, the USPTO's status and document system, and confirm the owner, whether the registration is live, and which goods or services it covers. If there's no number, try the USPTO trademark search for the name (USPTO).

Then compare their mark to yours the way a court would. The USPTO says the key factors in most infringement cases are how similar the marks are and whether the goods or services are related enough that buyers would wrongly assume they come from the same source. Courts also look at where each side sells, who the buyers are, any evidence of actual confusion, your intent when you picked the name, and how strong their mark is (USPTO, About trademark infringement).

Don't stop at "they never registered it." Federal registration isn't required for trademark rights. Someone who has used a name for particular goods can have common-law rights, and those rights can survive even if a registration expired or was cancelled (USPTO). The flip side helps you too. If you were selling under your name first, your own use counts.

Pull together your own evidence

Priority matters a lot here. To win an infringement case, the trademark owner has to prove it owns a valid mark and that its rights are senior to yours (USPTO). So find the earliest proof you have of selling under your name. Old order emails, first listing screenshots, invoices and dated social posts are all worth saving. Keep the letter and every follow-up message too. An attorney will want to see all of it, and it's a lot cheaper to hand over an organized folder than to pay someone to dig.

Your options, according to the USPTO

The USPTO lays out a handful of ways to respond and recommends an attorney experienced in trademark disputes because each one has legal consequences (USPTO).

You can reply and deny infringement if you have a basis for it. You can also reply asking for more specific evidence, like their dates of first use, whether the mark is federally registered, and where they've actually used it.

You can stay silent. The USPTO notes that some letters are sent hoping people will be scared into stopping or paying when they don't need to. Silence has a cost, though. If you're later found liable, a court may decide you acted recklessly and add to the damages.

You can negotiate. That might mean a license to keep using the name, or an agreement that your use doesn't infringe.

Or you can go on offense with a declaratory judgment lawsuit asking a court to say your mark doesn't infringe theirs. The USPTO also points out that you can challenge their registration at the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board with a petition to cancel, or oppose a pending application within 30 days after it's published in the Official Gazette (USPTO).

It helps to know what's at stake if it ever went to court. If an owner proves infringement, remedies can include an order to stop using the mark, destruction of infringing goods, the defendant's profits and damages, and in some cases attorneys' fees. A court can also find you don't infringe at all (USPTO).

When the "letter" comes from Etsy

Sometimes the first sign of trouble is a removed listing. Etsy's policy is to remove or disable content when it gets a report of alleged infringement that complies with its rules, and you'll get an email with the contact information of the person who filed it. Etsy says it doesn't decide which listings infringe and can't speak for the IP owner. It suggests contacting the reporting party directly or talking to an attorney, and asks you not to relist the same content until the issue is resolved, since repeat reports can cost you account privileges (Etsy Help).

Know which tool fits. Etsy describes its DMCA counter notice as a process for copyright reports (Etsy Help), so it may not be the right route for a trademark takedown. For trademark reports, read Etsy's Intellectual Property Policy for the current options. One common path is asking the person who reported you to withdraw the report.

Finding an attorney

You don't have to hire a lawyer to answer a cease and desist letter. Still, the USPTO says one can help you understand the scope of your rights and the weak spots in the claim against you, and can save you from bigger problems later. It suggests looking through your state bar association's attorney directory for a US-licensed attorney who focuses on trademark litigation (USPTO). Bring your evidence folder and a copy of the letter to the first call.

Seeing conflicts earlier

If the sender's own mark turns out to be a pending application, watch its status in TSDR. Once an application is published in the Official Gazette, there's a 30-day window to oppose it at the TTAB (USPTO, Approval for publication). Spotting a similar filing during that window gives you a formal way to object before it ever registers.

FAQ

Does a cease and desist letter mean I'm being sued?
No. A lawsuit generally comes with a served complaint and summons. A letter by itself doesn't mean a case was filed (USPTO).
Can I just ignore it?
You can, but if you're later found liable, a court may treat that as reckless and award more damages (USPTO).
What if the sender doesn't have a registered trademark?
They can still have common-law rights from using the name with their goods or services (USPTO). Compare their first use dates with yours.
Can I file a counter notice if Etsy removed my listing for trademark?
Possibly not. Etsy describes counter notices as a copyright process. Check Etsy's Intellectual Property Policy for current options, such as asking the reporter to withdraw the report.
