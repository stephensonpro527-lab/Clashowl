You found a pending US application for a name that's way too close to yours. It hasn't registered yet, which is good news, because the USPTO gives outsiders two ways to push back before it does. One is cheap and quiet and works only for certain problems. The other is a real legal case. Picking the wrong one, or picking the right one too late, is how small sellers lose names they've used for years.

Start by finding where the application is

Look the application up in the USPTO trademark search and check its status. What matters most is whether it has been published yet. After an examiner approves a mark, it's published in the weekly Trademark Official Gazette, and publication starts a 30-day window when anyone who thinks they'd be harmed can file a Notice of Opposition with the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (USPTO, Approval for publication). The Gazette comes out every Tuesday (USPTO, Trademark Official Gazette), so the clock always starts on a Tuesday.

If nobody opposes or asks for more time within those 30 days, the mark moves on to registration, or to a notice of allowance if it was filed on intent to use (USPTO, Choosing your ESTTA form).

The letter of protest

A letter of protest lets you hand the USPTO evidence about why someone else's pending application shouldn't register. You don't argue with the applicant and you don't become a party. Only your evidence gets forwarded to the examining attorney, and your letter and arguments don't (USPTO, Letter of protest practice tip). The fee is $150 (USPTO fee schedule), and you file it with the TEAS Letter of Protest form, one letter per application.

The catch is that it only covers reasons an examiner can act on alone. The USPTO lists common ones, like the mark being likely to be confused with a US registration or an earlier pending application, or the mark being merely descriptive or generic for the goods (USPTO). So it fits well if you already own a registration or filed your own application first.

Here's the part that trips up a lot of Etsy and Shopify sellers. The USPTO says it typically won't consider a letter of protest that claims common law prior use, meaning you used the name first but never registered it. It also won't take evidence of actual customer confusion or declarations, since those belong in a TTAB proceeding (USPTO). If your claim is "I was selling under this name before they filed," a letter of protest probably isn't your tool.

Timing matters too. The USPTO asks you to file as soon as you can after the application is filed, ideally before publication. After publication your evidence has to make a prima facie case, which is a higher bar, and a letter filed after the 30-day opposition period usually won't be considered at all. Filing one also doesn't pause anything. It doesn't give you more time to oppose, so if the mark publishes while your letter is pending you still need to file an opposition or extension on time (USPTO).

If you go this route, keep it tight. The USPTO caps evidence at 10 items per reason and 75 pages total, wants an itemized index on a separate page, and needs webpage evidence to show the URL and the date you accessed it. For a confusion claim, it only looks at the first five registrations or applications you list. Any evidence it considers becomes public, so redact personal details first (USPTO).

The opposition

An opposition is a trial case at the TTAB. You file a notice of opposition in TTAB Center with a MyUSPTO account, attach a pleading and pay the fee. The pleading uses short numbered paragraphs stating facts that would show you have standing and a legal ground for opposing. You don't have to prove them at filing, but you need a good faith belief they're true (USPTO, Initiating a new proceeding).

This is where unregistered prior use can count. If you claim prior rights in a common law mark that isn't in any application, the form asks you to attach an image of the mark, or you can type it in if it's words only (USPTO). Dated proof of your first sales, old listings and packaging is what backs that claim up later.

The fee is $600 per class when filed electronically (USPTO fee schedule). If the application covers three classes and you oppose all of them, that's $1,800 before any lawyer. Talking to an attorney first makes sense here. You'll be in an adversarial proceeding with discovery and briefs, and the TTAB lists most of those filings as electronic only (USPTO, Filing with TTAB).

Buying time with an extension

Thirty days goes fast when you're also running a shop. You can ask for more time, but the first request has to go in before those 30 days end. Your first request can be 30 days, granted on request, or 90 days for good cause. After a 30-day extension you can ask for 60 more for good cause. One final 60-day extension is available only with the applicant's consent or extraordinary circumstances, and nothing beyond that is granted (USPTO, Choosing your ESTTA form). Added up, that's 180 days after publication at most.

Fees for those requests, filed electronically, are $0 for the first 30 days, $200 for a first 90-day or second 60-day request and $400 for the final 60 days (USPTO fee schedule). The free 30-day extension is often the smartest first move, since it gets you time to talk to the other side or a lawyer.

Two gotchas. You can't ride on someone else's extension, so if another company got more time you still have to file your own within the original 30 days (USPTO, Opposition period and extensions of time to oppose). And an extension belongs to the person who got it, so the opposition has to be filed in that same name (USPTO). File as your LLC if your LLC is the one selling. Also note the TTAB goes by Eastern Time for filing dates (USPTO), which matters if you're filing late at night on the West Coast.

Which one should you use?

If the application hasn't published and you have a registration or an earlier application of your own, a letter of protest is cheap and worth trying early. If your rights come from using the name without registering, you're probably looking at an opposition, and the window opens the Tuesday the mark appears in the Gazette. Some sellers do both, filing a protest early and still watching for publication so they can oppose or extend in time.

If the mark already registered, you've missed both. A petition for cancellation can be filed at any time, though after five years of registration only the grounds in Trademark Act section 14 are available (USPTO). FAQ

What's the difference between a letter of protest and an opposition?
A letter of protest sends evidence to the examining attorney and doesn't make you a party. An opposition is a TTAB proceeding against the applicant, filed after publication (USPTO).
I used the name first but never registered it. Can I file a letter of protest?
Usually not. The USPTO says it typically doesn't consider letters of protest based on common law prior use (USPTO). An opposition is where that claim goes.
How long do I have to oppose?
Thirty days after publication in the Official Gazette, with extensions that can't go past 180 days and must be requested in time (USPTO).
What does it cost?
$150 for a letter of protest and $600 per class for an electronic notice of opposition, per the USPTO fee schedule. Attorney fees are separate.
