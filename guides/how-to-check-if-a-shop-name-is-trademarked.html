You've found a name you love, the Etsy shop name is free, and you're about to order labels. Before you do, spend half an hour finding out whether someone already has trademark rights in it. Renaming a shop after it has reviews and printed packaging costs far more than that half hour.

Why the domain and shop name being free proves nothing

Marketplaces and domain registrars will happily give you a name that someone else owns as a trademark. They aren't checking. Your LLC or DBA filing doesn't check either. The USPTO is clear that a trademark gives rights in how a word is used with specific goods or services, and that you become an owner by using it, with registration adding nationwide rights (USPTO, What is a trademark?). So the question to answer is whether anyone is already using or has filed for something close to your name for products like yours.

Search the USPTO database

The USPTO runs a free trademark search system covering federally registered and pending marks. The USPTO's own search page links to a getting started handout if the interface feels unfamiliar, and it suggests signing in to a USPTO.gov account to avoid errors when traffic is heavy.

Search the exact name first, then try it the way a customer might mishear or misspell it. If your name is two words, search each word on its own too. A hit on "Willow" matters when you're planning "Willow & Wick" candles.

For each result, open the record and read the goods and services. Note whether the filing is pending or registered. A pending application that was filed before yours could still block you later. The USPTO says examiners will warn an applicant when an earlier-filed pending application for related goods might lead to refusal if that earlier mark registers (USPTO, Likelihood of confusion).

What counts as too close

This part trips people up. A name doesn't have to match letter for letter to cause trouble. The USPTO says marks can be confusingly similar in sound, appearance, meaning or overall commercial impression, and that likelihood of confusion is the most common reason it refuses registration (USPTO, Likelihood of confusion). Swapping a "c" for a "k" or adding "Co" at the end won't help much if the name still sounds the same out loud.

The other half of the test is the goods. The USPTO uses Dove soap and Dove ice cream bars as an example of identical marks owned by different companies, because the products aren't related. Goods can count as related when they're used together, sold to the same buyers, advertised together or sold by the same kind of dealer. One of the USPTO's own prompts is to picture what sits in the same store aisle as your product (USPTO). If you sell soy candles, a registered mark for room sprays or wax melts deserves a close look. One for accounting software probably doesn't.

If you're unsure how your products are usually described, the USPTO Trademark ID Manual shows the standard wording for goods and services, which makes it easier to compare your listings with what's in a record.

Look past federal filings

A clean USPTO search isn't the end. Someone can own rights in a name just by using it, without ever filing, and the USPTO says that earlier common-law use can affect your rights even if you register later. It recommends searching the internet for the name and similar names used with related goods (USPTO, Comprehensive clearance search).

For a small seller that means searching Etsy and Amazon for your name plus your product type, then checking Google with and without quotes. Look at the USPTO's page of state trademark and business registry links for the states where you sell most. The USPTO's clearance page also lists the weekly Trademark Official Gazette, and says that at a minimum you should check its database and the Gazette and search the internet for common-law use (USPTO).

Reading what you found

If nothing close turns up for related goods, you're in a good spot. Keep notes with dates and screenshots of what you searched, since you may want them later.

If you find the same or a very similar name on related products, pick a different name now. It's the cheapest moment you'll ever have to change it.

The gray zone is a similar name on products that might or might not be related. That's a good time for a short consult. The USPTO says an attorney can run a clearance search and interpret the results, and that screening services exist for deeper searches (USPTO). Be careful with anyone who claims a USPTO endorsement, because the USPTO doesn't recommend specific companies or attorneys (USPTO, Recognizing common scams).

FAQ

Where do I check if a name is trademarked in the US?
The free USPTO trademark search covers registered and pending federal marks. Add a web and marketplace search for unregistered use (USPTO).
Can I use a name if nobody registered it?
Not always. Rights start with use, even without registration, though they're limited to where the owner sells (USPTO).
Can two shops have the same name?
Sometimes, if the products are unrelated. The USPTO's example is Dove soap and Dove ice cream bars (USPTO).
Does a different spelling get around a trademark?
Usually not. Marks that sound alike or give the same impression can still be confusingly similar (USPTO).
