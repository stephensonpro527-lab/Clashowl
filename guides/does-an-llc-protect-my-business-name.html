Lots of small business owners do everything that feels official. They search the Secretary of State database, form the LLC, maybe file a DBA, buy the domain, and assume the name is theirs. Then a cease and desist arrives from a business they've never heard of. The short answer to the question is no. A state business filing and a trademark are different things, handled by different offices, and they protect different things.

What your LLC or DBA filing actually does

The US Small Business Administration lays out four separate ways to register a name and says each one is "legally independent." An entity name, like your LLC or corporation, protects you at the state level and in most cases prevents anyone else in that state from operating under the same entity name. A DBA "doesn't give legal protection, but it might be legally required." A domain name protects your website address. Only a trademark protects a name at the federal level (SBA, Choose your business name).

The USPTO draws the same line. A trade name is "simply the name of your business," the one you register with your state to conduct business there. A trademark is a word, name, symbol or design that identifies the source of your goods or services, and you register it with the USPTO to secure nationwide ownership rights (USPTO, How trademarks and trade names differ).

So the state approving "Willow & Wick Candle Co., LLC" tells you nobody else has that exact entity name in your state. It tells you nothing about whether someone in another state owns a trademark on WILLOW & WICK for candles.

Where trademark rights come from

You don't need to register to have some trademark rights. The USPTO says you become a trademark owner as soon as you start using the mark with your goods or services, but those rights are limited and "only apply to the geographic area in which you're providing your goods or services." Registering with the USPTO is what creates nationwide rights (USPTO, What is a trademark?).

There's also a middle option. A state trademark registration, which is different from your LLC filing, creates rights in that state only, and the USPTO notes that not every state even has a trademark database (USPTO, Why register your trademark?).

Why this catches online sellers

If you sell on Etsy, Amazon or Shopify, your customers are everywhere, so a name that was only cleared at the state level is exposed in every other state. Someone with a federal registration for a confusingly similar mark on related goods can come after you, and the name doesn't have to be identical. A Charlotte trademark attorney told Axios that registering an LLC with the state doesn't give a business exclusive trademark protection over its name, and that whether two businesses offer similar goods or services factors into whether names conflict (Axios Charlotte, Aug. 28, 2026).

The same thing works the other way. Marketplace brand programs and IP reporting tools generally rely on trademark filings, not state business filings. Our guide on trademarking your Etsy shop name covers that in more detail.

What to do before and after you form the LLC

Before you commit to a name, search more than your state's database. The USPTO encourages searching the internet, state trademark databases and business name databases, because its own database only covers federal applications and registrations and leaves out people with common law rights (USPTO). Then search the federal database itself in the USPTO trademark search, including sound-alike spellings and names used for related products. Our step-by-step guide on checking if a shop name is trademarked walks through it.

After you launch, keep records of when and where you first used the name, since those dates are your evidence of common law rights. If the name matters to the business, consider a federal application. The base USPTO application fee is listed per class of goods or services in the USPTO fee schedule, and an attorney can help you pick the right classes.

Finally, keep an eye on new filings. A state filing won't warn you when someone applies for a trademark close to your name, and the window to oppose a published application is only 30 days (see letter of protest vs. opposition). FAQ

Does forming an LLC protect my business name?
Only at the state entity level. The SBA says it usually prevents anyone else in your state from operating under the same entity name, but it isn't a trademark (SBA).
Does a DBA give me rights to a name?
No. The SBA says a DBA doesn't give legal protection, though you may be required to file one (SBA).
Where do my rights come from if I never register a trademark?
From actually using the name to sell. Those common law rights are limited to the area where you do business, while a federal registration covers the whole country (USPTO).
The state approved my LLC name. Doesn't that mean it's available?
No. State business filings and USPTO trademarks are separate systems, so check the federal trademark database too (USPTO).
