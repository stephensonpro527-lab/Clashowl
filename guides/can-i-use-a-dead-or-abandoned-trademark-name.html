You picked a name for your Etsy shop, Amazon brand or Shopify store, searched the USPTO database, and found it. The good news is the record says DEAD. Does that mean the name is free? Sometimes. A dead record tells you about a federal filing, not about whether anyone is still using the name. Here's how to read what you found and what to check before you print labels.

What "dead" means in the USPTO database

Every application and registration in the USPTO's records is either live or dead. A record usually goes dead for one of two reasons.

The application was abandoned. The USPTO says an abandoned application is no longer live and can't mature into a registration, and that this can happen for many reasons, for example missing the deadline to respond to an office action (USPTO, Reviving an abandoned application). Many small filers simply stop responding.

The registration was canceled or expired. A registration has to be maintained. The USPTO says owners must file a declaration of use between the fifth and sixth years after registration, then a declaration and renewal between the ninth and tenth years, and every ten years after that. There's a six-month grace period, and if nothing is filed by the end of it, the registration is canceled or expires (USPTO, Keeping your registration alive).

You can open any record in TSDR by serial or registration number to see its status, the reason it died and the date.

Why a dead record doesn't always mean a free name

Federal registration is optional. The USPTO says you aren't required to register a trademark, and that owners can rely on "common law" rights that come from using the mark in commerce in a particular geographic area. It also says its search database is limited to federal applications and registrations and doesn't include people who have trademark rights but no federal registration (USPTO, Why register your trademark?).

So picture a candle maker who registered a name, forgot to file the five-year declaration, and kept selling. Their registration is dead in the database, but they may still have common law rights where they sell. If you launch similar candles under the same name, a dead registration won't protect you from a dispute with them.

The reverse also happens. An application might have died because the owner gave up on the brand entirely. In that case the name may well be open, but you'd still want to confirm it from real-world evidence, not just the status line.

When the law treats a mark as abandoned

"Abandoned" in the USPTO's status and "abandoned" in trademark law aren't the same thing. Under the Lanham Act, a mark is abandoned when its use has been discontinued with intent not to resume, and the statute says nonuse for 3 consecutive years is prima facie evidence of abandonment (15 U.S.C. § 1127). That turns on what the owner is actually doing in the market, which is why the next step matters.

What to check before you use the name

  • Read the record. In TSDR, note the owner, the goods and services, and when and why the record died. A registration canceled last month is a very different situation from an application abandoned ten years ago.
  • Look for current use. Search the name with the old goods on Google, Etsy, Amazon, Shopify stores and social media. Check whether the owner's website still sells under it. The USPTO recommends searching the internet, state trademark databases and business name databases for similar marks (USPTO).
  • Check whether it can come back. The USPTO lets applicants petition to revive an abandoned application, generally no later than two months after the Notice of Abandonment, or within two months of learning of it and no later than six months after the abandonment date in TSDR (USPTO). A very recent abandonment can still change.
  • Search for live look-alikes too. A dead record for your exact name doesn't mean there's no live filing for a similar spelling or a sound-alike in related goods. Our guide on checking whether a shop name is trademarked walks through that search.

If a live registration is blocking you but nobody uses it

Sometimes the problem is the opposite: the record is live, but the owner doesn't seem to sell anything under the name. The USPTO offers expungement (for marks never used with some or all of the listed goods) and reexamination (for marks not in use by the required date). Each costs $400 per class. Expungement can be requested between three and ten years after registration, and reexamination within the first five years. A TTAB cancellation proceeding, which can also raise abandonment, costs $600 per class (USPTO, Requesting an expungement or reexamination proceeding). These take evidence and time, so this is a good point to talk to a trademark attorney.

If the live record is your examiner's reason for refusing your own application, that is a likelihood of confusion office action, which you can respond to. The USPTO says a dead cited registration can no longer block yours.

FAQ

The USPTO says my name is DEAD. Is it free to use?
Not automatically. The federal filing is gone, but the old owner may still have common law rights where they use the name, and those aren't in the USPTO database (USPTO). Check for current use first.
Can I file a trademark application for a name that has a dead record?
A dead record doesn't block a new application the way a live one can, but the examiner will still look at live marks that are similar, and a prior user could still oppose or challenge you.
Can an abandoned application come back to life?
Yes, sometimes. The applicant can petition to revive, generally within two months of the Notice of Abandonment and no later than six months after the abandonment date in TSDR. The petition fee is $250 for electronic filing (USPTO, fee schedule).
How long does someone have to stop using a mark before it's abandoned?
There's no fixed cutoff, but the Lanham Act says nonuse for 3 consecutive years is prima facie evidence of abandonment, and abandonment also requires intent not to resume use (15 U.S.C. § 1127).
What does it cost to challenge an unused registration?
A petition for expungement or reexamination is $400 per class, and a TTAB cancellation is $600 per class (USPTO).
